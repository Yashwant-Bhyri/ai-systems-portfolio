# Elite Narrative Lock — Astra Working Brief

Date: 2026-09-22
Status: supporting direction for the active Astra portfolio task

## Read this as a design and narrative contract

This document is not a request to replace the existing portfolio, discard the current checkpoint, or add more feature labels. It is the reasoning contract for the next iteration of the two flagship compute chapters.

Preserve the strongest existing work, especially the directed execution reveal, the test-time scaling practical reveal, the policy films, and the playback behavior. The goal is to make the whole experience cohere as one technical argument.

Do not begin by adding isolated SVG effects. First make sure the story, state model, and causal sequence are correct. The visual polish should be generated by a coherent technical world rather than used to cover a fragmented one.

## The actual product thesis

The portfolio is a technical-authority instrument for technically literate second-degree audiences: engineering managers, senior systems engineers, research leads, and technical peers.

It should make a visitor think:

> This person understands important AI systems deeply, can reason about their tradeoffs, and can probably build them. I want to test them further.

The project is not primarily a research paper, a benchmark report, or a generic interactive dashboard. It is a visually unforgettable technical demonstration that compresses serious systems into inspectable mechanisms.

The desired sequence is:

```text
attention → intuition → respect → technical curiosity → interview opportunity
```

The portfolio must work at several depths:

| Depth | Visitor question | Surface |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 seconds | What does this person work on? | High-level architecture |
| 3 minutes | What did this person understand or build? | Component microscope |
| 7–10 minutes | Can this person reason about tradeoffs? | Results laboratory |

## What “elite engineering” means here

For this project, elite engineering is not only measured performance. It is:

```text
technical depth × visual compression × deliberate taste × memorable interaction
```

The target reference is the feeling of a Three Blue One Brown explanation combined with a private systems lab and the finish of a premium product demo.

Recommended proportions for the Results Laboratory:

- 60% authored technical film;
- 25% interactive inspection;
- 15% research-style readout and methodology.

The film is the spine. Controls are there to inspect causality. The result layer gives the work authority. Do not let the control surface become the experience.

## The shared narrative grammar

Every important scene should follow the same six beats:

1. Establish the state.
2. Isolate the bottleneck.
3. Apply one intervention.
4. Show the direct visual delta.
5. Show the tradeoff.
6. Carry the changed state into the next scene.

The visitor must never wonder what changed, what stayed fixed, why the movement is happening, or whether the next result came from the previous intervention.

Use persistent visual identities. Requests, workers, KV pages, tokens, matrices, task identities, policy state, and result traces should continue across scenes where possible. Do not reset the world after every chapter.

### Spotlight without collapse

Highlighting one mechanism must never remove the rest of the system. Spotlight by contrast, scale, light, motion, or focus. Keep the surrounding architecture visible but quiet.

The rule is:

> Spotlight one mechanism; preserve the system around it.

## The three-act structure for each flagship

### Act 1 — Architecture: the promise

Answer in approximately 30 seconds:

- What system is this?
- What problem does it solve?
- What did the author contribute?
- What flows through it?

### Act 2 — Microscope: the mechanism

Answer in approximately 3 minutes:

- What does each important component do?
- What state changes at runtime?
- What is input, operation, and output?
- Which implementation decisions matter?

### Act 3 — Results Laboratory: the consequence

Answer in approximately 7–10 minutes:

- What fails or becomes expensive under pressure?
- What intervention changes the behavior?
- What stays fixed?
- What tradeoff appears?
- What does the intervention enable in a real system?

The Results Laboratory should not feel like an appended chart gallery. It is the third act of the system explanation.

# Inference Optimization Engine

## The locked central question

> How do we serve the same intelligence under increasing load without paying for the same work repeatedly?

“Serving under load” is the engineering problem. KV reuse, speculative decoding, scheduling, kernels, and GPU placement are the mechanisms inside that problem.

Do not lead with “What is speculative decoding?” or “What is KV-cache reuse?” Those are too narrow as opening questions.

## The intended causal sequence

```text
load arrives
→ queue pressure appears
→ scheduling changes waiting
→ prefix reuse removes repeated prefill
→ speculation reduces target-model steps
→ execution optimization lowers per-step cost
→ capacity is tested at scale
→ all interventions compose
```

## Scene 0 — The workload arrives

Open with a live serving environment:

- requests arriving over time;
- different prompt and output lengths;
- shared-prefix groups;
- active concurrency;
- workers and GPUs becoming occupied.

The visitor should first feel the system under pressure, not see a chart.

Suggested framing:

> Same model. Same answers. More requests. Less wasted execution.

Use a small representative trace for the authored film. Use a later scale sweep for 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 requests or different concurrency levels. Do not put every scale on screen at once.

## Scene 1 — The baseline fails visibly

Show rigid or barrier-based serving:

- requests waiting for a partner;
- head-of-line blocking;
- idle worker gaps;
- queue growth;
- tail latency expansion.

The first lesson is:

> Serving quality is affected by scheduling, not only by model speed.

## Scene 2 — Release the queue

Introduce continuous or independent scheduling.

Show that:

- request work lengths remain similar;
- start times change;
- released workers receive the next request;
- queueing delay falls;
- p95 latency improves;
- busy GPU work may remain nearly unchanged.

This distinction is important:

> Lower latency does not necessarily mean less computation.

The result readout should appear after the scheduling intervention, comparing queue time, p50/p95 latency, worker occupancy, and busy GPU-seconds.

## Scene 3 — Remember the prefix

Follow one shared prefix through the runtime:

1. The first request performs a cold read.
2. KV pages are allocated.
3. The prefix becomes reusable state.
4. A later request references the pages.
5. Only the suffix enters prefill.

Show the crossover point where cache management becomes worthwhile. The important conclusion is not “cache good.” It is:

> The value of KV reuse depends on workload structure, readiness, and reuse frequency.

The current prefix story is useful. It should become part of the persistent load world rather than an isolated figure.

## Scene 4 — Draft, verify, commit

Speculative decoding needs its own causal beat in the Results Laboratory, not only a lower-level architecture animation.

Show:

```text
draft proposes: 1 2 3 4 5
target verifies: 1 2 3 4 5
accepted: 1 2 3 4
rejected: 5
fallback: continue normally
```

Expose the tradeoff:

```text
draft cost versus target-model work avoided
```

Metrics to make visible:

- accepted tokens per verification round;
- rejected suffix length;
- target-model passes avoided;
- draft overhead;
- fallback frequency;
- latency and throughput under changing batch conditions.

DSpark, EAGLE/EAGLE3, MTP, NGRAM, vLLM Speculators, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, and related systems should be shown as implementation lineage after the mechanism is understood. They should not become a keyword wall.

## Scene 5 — Execute the remaining work efficiently

The existing `ExecutionReveal` is a strong direction and should be preserved.

The intended visual is two synchronized lanes:

```text
reference execution
optimized execution
```

Both receive the same request, input, output length, and answer. The optimized lane finishes first. When it finishes:

- the result locks;
- the reference lane continues in a contrasting state;
- a completion delta appears;
- cumulative GPU work continues separating.

Then repeat the profile at 1×, 10×, 100×, and 1,000×. Show the gap accumulating as busy work. Preserve the distinction between busy GPU work and wall-clock latency.

This is one of the signature moments of the chapter:

> Same answer. Same token work. Different execution burden.

## Scene 6 — Raise the system to cluster scale

This can be shorter than the core scenes, but it must be represented in the data model and the final system envelope.

Show the operating point changing across:

- one GPU;
- multiple GPUs;
- model-parallel execution;
- distributed request assignment.

Make the tradeoff visible:

> More GPUs increase capacity, but coordination and communication become part of the cost.

Do not imply a real cluster benchmark unless one exists. Keep the story at the level of an inspectable system model until actual GPU runs are available.

## Scene 7 — Compose the gains

Only after each mechanism has had its own causal scene should the final composition occur:

- scheduling removes waiting;
- KV reuse removes repeated prefill;
- speculation removes unnecessary target steps;
- execution optimization lowers the cost of remaining steps;
- placement and parallelism change capacity.

End with latency, throughput, GPU work, cache reuse, acceptance behavior, and correctness/output equivalence in one composed readout. The full cohort result should appear here, not remain visually dominant throughout the film.

# Advanced Post-Training

## The locked central question

> How does a reasoning policy improve, and how should it spend a finite amount of test-time compute?

The post-training project should be a policy-and-compute story, not a generic training animation.

## The intended causal sequence

```text
base policy
→ reasoning data
→ SFT / LoRA policy change
→ verifier-scored rollouts
→ relative reward and policy update
→ fixed-policy test-time search
→ efficiency-aware policy comparison
→ paired gain/loss audit
```

## Base policy

Start with one fixed task cohort and visible task identities. Begin with a few representative tasks, then zoom out to the cohort.

The visitor should understand that all later comparisons preserve task identity.

## SFT + LoRA

Preserve the policy-parameter-change SVG as the teaching foundation:

```text
frozen W + trainable B × A = adapted policy
```

Start with a concrete reasoning structure:

```text
read → decompose → solve → check
```

Then show the matrix update:

- W remains fixed;
- A and B receive gradients;
- ΔW = B × A;
- output behavior changes.

Then zoom out from the teaching example to the cohort. The matrix is not the final result. The changed reasoning behavior is the result.

## Rollouts and relative rewards

This should be the signature post-training mechanism:

```text
rollouts → verifier → rewards → group mean → relative advantages → policy update
```

Show several attempts for one task:

- incomplete;
- fluent but wrong;
- correct;
- correct but inefficient.

Then show how the update changes future policy probabilities. The visitor should understand the difference between sampled attempts, verifier signal, relative reward, and policy update.

DPO can remain an alternative alignment route, not a mandatory next chapter.

## More thought at inference time

Freeze the policy visibly. Only the reasoning allowance changes:

```text
1× → 2× → 4× → 8×
```

Show:

- more candidate paths;
- more verifier calls;
- higher cost;
- potential recovery of a missed solution;
- no weight update.

The core lesson is:

> More thought is not the same as a better policy.

The current practical repository/payment example is a strong direction and should be kept. It connects the compute curve to an actual user-facing failure mode while preserving the distinction between inference-time search and training.

## Efficiency-aware policy and negative flips

Compare policies at the same nominal allowance and expose:

- retained correct tasks;
- new successes;
- old-correct → new-wrong negative flips;
- work multiplier;
- cost-quality frontier.

Do not reduce the conclusion to “the new policy is better.” The stronger conclusion is:

> The policy changes the quality-cost frontier, and the gains must be inspected together with regressions.

# The final convergence

The two laboratories should end as two sides of one AI systems argument:

```text
post-training: improve what the policy can do
inference: deliver that policy more efficiently
```

The final conclusion is not a dashboard. It is an authored statement:

> A strong AI system needs both a better policy and a better runtime.

This gives the portfolio a coherent identity: the author understands the model layer and the serving layer, not only isolated applications around them.

# Evidence and presentation boundary

The primary film should not lead with blunt labels such as `SIMULATED MECHANISM` or `REAL BENCHMARK PENDING`. That is not the desired emotional experience.

However, the boundary must remain technically honest. Use quiet methodology language, an inspection layer, and precise verbs. Do not phrase synthetic scenario numbers as measured production achievements. Keep detailed assumptions, missing artifacts, and reproducibility information in the inspector or method contract.

The target is not “real versus pretty.” The target is:

- a conceptual mechanism that is clearly presented as a mechanism;
- an implemented subsystem shown as an implementation;
- a measured result shown as a measured result;
- a future benchmark shown as a future benchmark.

The cinematic surface can remain ambitious while the technical boundary remains available to a serious visitor.

# What the next iteration must not do

- Do not add more isolated keyword cards.
- Do not make every SOTA implementation a separate feature panel.
- Do not let the result dashboard remain permanently expanded.
- Do not reset the visual world between every chapter.
- Do not let the practical example become a caption below the mathematics.
- Do not remove the wider system when spotlighting one mechanism.
- Do not claim a runtime, benchmark, model, GPU count, or result that has not actually been run.
- Do not polish transitions indefinitely while the causal sequence remains structurally incomplete.

# Acceptance test for the next version

A cold technical visitor should be able to answer these questions after the guided film:

1. What problem is the system solving?
2. What stayed fixed?
3. What intervention changed the behavior?
4. What work, latency, quality, or memory state changed?
5. What tradeoff appeared?
6. How would this map to a real serving or post-training stack?
7. What would need to be benchmarked or verified in a real implementation?

If the visitor can only say “there were many polished animations,” the narrative has failed. If they can say “I understand the mechanism, the tradeoff, and what I would ask this candidate next,” the narrative is working.

## Final lock sentence

> Begin with a system under pressure. Make the bottleneck visible. Apply one mechanism at a time. Preserve the invariant. Show the direct delta. Carry the state forward. End with the composed system and the tradeoffs that remain.
